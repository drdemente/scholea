# Scholea User Guide

*A complete, student-focused guide to planning, running, and writing up any of 17
study types, to the right reporting standard.*

Scholea walks you, step by step, through a real research project: framing the
question, collecting and appraising evidence or data, analysing it with validated
tools, and compiling a first draft written to the correct **reporting standard**
(PRISMA, CONSORT, STROBE, STARD, and more). Everything you do is saved on your
device automatically, and you do not need an account.

This guide covers not only what to do on each screen, but **how to actually carry
out each step** of real research, in plain language. It is the same guide in the
desktop app and in the web version.

> **A note on honesty.** Scholea never claims to write a paper *for* you. Any
> AI-assisted text it produces is labelled a **"structured draft to a reporting
> standard,"** never "ready to submit." You are the author. Review every sentence,
> number, and citation.

## 1. Accounts (optional)

You do not need an account. Open Scholea and start a project: everything is saved
on this device, and the research steps work offline. An account adds two things.
Your projects sync between your devices, and you can share a project with other
people.

> If the top of the page shows neither a **Sign in** button nor your name, your
> copy of Scholea was built without accounts. Skip this section and the sharing
> part of §8. Everything else works the same.

### Create an account

1. Choose **Sign in** at the top of the page, then **Create an account**.
2. Enter your name, your email address and a password of at least 10 characters
   (you type the password twice), then choose **Create account**.
3. Scholea emails you a code. Type it into **Confirmation code** and choose
   **Confirm and sign in**. There is no link to click.

The email says how long the code lasts. If it does not arrive after a few minutes,
check your spam folder, then choose **Send a new code** (it becomes available
after a minute). If the address already has a Scholea account, no code is sent:
sign in, or reset your password, instead.

The name you enter is what the people you work with see beside your comments and
sign-offs. You can change it later under **Edit profile** in the account menu.

### Forgot your password

1. On the sign-in form choose **Forgot password?**, enter your email address and
   choose **Email me a code**.
2. Type the code from the email, choose a new password, and choose
   **Set password and sign in**.

### Invited by an administrator

Some organisations create accounts for their members. If an administrator invited
you, you received an email with an invitation code.

1. Choose **Sign in**, then **Have an invitation code?**.
2. Enter the email address the invitation was sent to, the code, and a password of
   your own, then choose **Set password and sign in**.

If the code has expired, choose **Code expired? Email me a new one** on the same
form.

### Choosing a password after a code

After any emailed code, Scholea makes sure the account has a password that you
chose. Usually you will not notice. If it shows **Choose a password**, save one to
continue: nothing syncs to the account until you do, and the only other way on is
**Sign out instead**. The same step may show the name and institution on the
account. Check that they are yours.

### Projects you made before signing in

Signing in never uploads anything by itself. After you sign in, the Projects page
tells you how many projects on this device are not in your account yet, and offers
two choices for them:

- **Add to my account** uploads them, so they sync to your other devices and can
  be shared.
- **Keep on this device only** leaves them where they are. They are never
  uploaded, and they stay visible whether or not you are signed in.

You can change your mind later for one project at a time: open it, choose
**Share**, then **Add to my account**. Projects you create while you are signed in
go to your account automatically.

### What the label on each project means

When accounts are available, each project on the Projects page carries a label:

| Label | What it means |
|-------|---------------|
| **This device only** | Saved on this device and nowhere else. |
| **Not uploaded yet** | On its way into your account, but its content has not been uploaded yet. It uploads the next time Scholea syncs. |
| **In your account** | Your own project, stored in your account and synced. |
| **Shared with you** | Someone else's project, which you joined by accepting an invitation. |

Inside an open project, a badge at the top says **Saved on this device** or
**Synced to your account**.

### The account menu

When you are signed in, your name or initial appears at the top of the page.
Choose it to open the account menu. Under your name, a line says when your
projects last synced, or that you are offline or that a sync did not finish.
Below that:

- **Sync now** syncs straight away. Scholea also syncs by itself: when you sign in,
  shortly after you edit, when your device comes back online, and when you return
  to the window.
- **Invitations** lists the project invitations waiting for you (see §8).
- **Edit profile** changes your name and institution.
- **Change password** asks for your current password, then the new one.
- **Sign out** signs this device out.

Three more entries appear only when they apply: **Sign in again** (the account
service no longer accepts this device's session, so nothing syncs until you do),
**Confirm your email address** (an administrator changed your address, and until
you confirm it with a code, project invitations sent to it do not reach you), and
**Contact support** (your organisation set a support address).

### Signing out

**Sign out** hides the projects from your account on this device until you sign in
again. Nothing is deleted: they are still in your account, and projects labelled
**This device only** stay visible. Scholea tries one last upload before it signs
out. Changes that could not be uploaded are kept on this device and upload the
next time you sign in here.

### If your organisation runs Scholea

If your account belongs to an organisation's Scholea service, its administrator
can set the organisation's name (shown beside the Scholea name), its support
contact (shown in Settings and, in the app, at the bottom of this guide), an
accent colour, and which study types are offered for **new** projects. These apply
only while you are signed in, and existing projects are never affected.

Accounts and these settings are managed outside the app. Scholea itself has no
administration screens, whatever role your account has.

## 2. The Projects home

When the app opens you land on **Projects**, your workspace hub.

- **Every saved project** appears as a card showing its title, study type, the
  reporting standard it targets, and a **progress bar** (completed steps / total).
- A **status marker** sits above each name: a tick and **Completed** (all steps
  done), or a clock and **In progress**.
- The project you're currently working on is tagged **ACTIVE**.
- **Continue / Review** opens a project and resumes at the first unfinished step.
- **New study** lets you pick a study type (grouped by family).
- **Delete** (with a confirmation) removes a project from this device. For a
  project in your account, the confirmation says what else happens: deleting a
  project you own deletes it for everyone it is shared with, and deleting a
  project that was shared with you only takes you out of it.

You can keep **many projects at once** and switch between them freely; each keeps
its own independent progress. Use **"All projects"** in the header (or the ⌘K
command palette → *Go to Projects*) to return here from inside a project.

If accounts are available in your copy of Scholea, this page also shows:

- **A note, once,** that an account is there if you want one. It offers
  **Create account**, **Sign in** and **Not now**, and you never have to answer.
- **A label on each project** that says where it lives (see §1).
- **After you sign in**, the question about projects that were already on this
  device (see §1).
- **Invitations** to other people's projects, with **Accept** and **Decline**
  (see §8).

## 3. Anatomy of a step

Every study type is a sequence of **steps**. Each step screen has the same layout,
so once you learn one you know them all.

- **Title & subtitle**: what this step is and why it matters.
- **Guidance panel**: plain-language instructions, worked examples, tips (marked
  **Tip**) and warnings (marked **Watch out**). The depth adjusts to your
  complexity level (see §4).
- **Glossary terms**: the underlined terms beside **Key terms** (e.g. *PICO*,
  *Cohen's kappa*) show a definition when you click them.
- **Inputs**: the fields you fill in. They are short text, long text, numbers,
  dropdowns, checkboxes, and **tag fields** (type a value, press **Enter**,
  repeat). Some inputs appear only at higher complexity levels.
- **AI writing help**: once you have added an AI key, click into any text box and
  a small ✨ icon appears in its corner; click it to have Scholea draft or refine
  that field for you (see §7).
- **Interactive tool** *(some steps)*: a purpose-built workspace such as the
  literature search, screening interface, appraisal matrix, meta-analysis, or a
  flow-diagram builder (see §6).
- **Validation**: Scholea checks your entries as you go, and again when you try
  to finish a step. **Errors** (red) must be fixed; **warnings** (yellow) are
  advisory and don't block you.
- **Mark complete & continue** records your progress and unlocks the next step.
  Steps are meant to be done in order; you can always go **Back** to revise.

Everything is **saved automatically** as you work, each time you leave a field or
move to another step. There is no save button to remember.

## 4. Complexity levels

A **complexity selector** (the Basic / Medium / Advanced switch at the top of an
open project, or *Set detail level* in the ⌘K palette) switches the whole app
between three depths:

| Level | Who it's for | What changes |
|-------|--------------|--------------|
| **Basic** | First-timers, undergrads | Plain-language guidance, only the essential fields. |
| **Medium** | Postgrads, guided coursework | More methodological nuance, extra optional fields, worked examples. |
| **Advanced** | Experienced researchers | Full depth, advanced fields (models, estimators, sensitivity analyses), rigorous warnings. |

Raising the level reveals more optional inputs and deeper guidance; lowering it
hides them again. Your answers are never deleted when you switch. Start at **Basic**
if you're unsure. You can move up at any time.

## 5. Settings & your API keys

Open **Settings** from the header or ⌘K. Scholea is **bring-your-own-key**: it
ships with no keys, and where an outside service needs one, the key is yours.
Nothing in Settings is required for the planning and writing steps, and the
literature search works without a key. A key is needed only for the AI writing
help. Two search sources take an optional key that raises their limits.

- **Contact email** is optional. It is sent with your searches to Crossref,
  OpenAlex and PubMed, and Crossref and OpenAlex give requests that carry an
  email faster, more reliable service. It is also offered as the contact address
  when you compile a paper or the ethics documents.
- **Literature sources** are the five databases the search step can query. Tick
  the ones you want; Europe PMC, Crossref and OpenAlex are ticked to begin with.
  **Europe PMC**, **Crossref** and **ClinicalTrials.gov** need no key.
  **OpenAlex** needs none either, and your contact email or a free OpenAlex key
  raises its limits. **PubMed** takes an optional free NCBI key for higher
  limits. It is searchable directly in the desktop app; the web version needs a
  PubMed proxy address, which Settings asks for.
- **Manual sources** (Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, WHO ICTRP and conference
  abstracts) have no public search API. Settings says how to export from each,
  and you bring the records in with **Choose RIS file** in the search step.
- **AI assistance** is optional. Choose **Anthropic** or
  **OpenAI-compatible**, and add your key. For a service other than Anthropic or
  OpenAI themselves, also enter its Base URL (most end in `/v1`). Turn on
  **Auto-fetch models** to pick the model from a live list pulled from your
  endpoint, or leave it off and type a model name yourself. With Anthropic no
  model is filled in to begin with, so do one or the other. This powers the ✨
  writing help. Without a key, the ✨ icon does not appear. (See §7.)
- **Identity on this device** is the name and role (researcher / supervisor) used
  for comments, sign-offs and activity on projects kept only on this device.
  Projects in your account use your account name instead, and your role on each
  project decides whether you can sign off (see §8).
- **About this workspace** appears when you are signed in to an organisation that
  has set its name or contact details.

Light / dark mode has its own button next to Settings, and the complexity level
has its own switch (see §4).

> **Security:** enter your keys yourself. Scholea stores them only on this device
> and never syncs them to an account. They are not encrypted there, so anyone who
> can use your browser profile or your user account on the computer can read
> them. Never paste a key you found in a web page or document.

## 6. The interactive tools

Certain steps hand you a dedicated workspace instead of plain fields:

- **Literature search**: search several databases at once from your question's
  keywords. **Filters** let you set a custom **time frame** (from–to years), how
  many results to pull **per source** (25 up to **All**, capped at 1000 to respect
  the free databases), **open-access only**, and an **article type** (RCTs /
  reviews). Every result carries a **coloured tag showing which database it came
  from**; filters a particular database can't honour are noted rather than silently
  skipped.
- **Import & deduplicate**: merges imported records and removes duplicates (matched
  by DOI/title) so each study is screened once.
- **Screening interface**: review each record's title/abstract with the keyboard
  (**include**, **exclude**, **undo**). A live counter shows **how many you've
  included, excluded, and have left**. Turn on a **second reviewer** to screen
  independently and watch a live **Cohen's kappa** agreement score.
- **Full-text eligibility list**: every study you included at screening appears in
  a checklist; confirm each (**Include**) or **Exclude it with its own recorded
  reason** (with common PRISMA reasons suggested). The excluded list, reasons, and
  counts flow straight into your PRISMA diagram.
- **Appraisal matrix**: a risk-of-bias grid listing **your actual included
  studies**; click a cell to cycle its judgement. With an AI key set,
  **AI-assess all studies** drafts per-domain ratings from each study's title and
  abstract, and from its open-access full text where one is found. The result is
  clearly labelled a draft to verify against the full text, and it never
  replaces a judgement you set yourself.
- **Synthesis / meta-analysis**: **your included studies are pulled in
  automatically** as rows, listed **alphabetically**. The input columns **follow
  the outcome type you chose at data extraction**: a *continuous* outcome asks for
  means, SDs and group sizes (pooled as a standardised or raw mean difference); a
  *dichotomous* outcome asks for events and totals (pooled as a risk ratio or odds
  ratio). Pooling runs by itself as soon as the data are valid, drawing a **forest
  plot** and **funnel plot** with heterogeneity (I², τ²) and an optional cross-check
  against the gold-standard `metafor` package. The forest plot lists **each
  study's weight** and shows the **pooled estimate with its p-value** (test of
  overall effect); it scales to your window without being cropped.
- **Flow-diagram builders**: generate a publication-ready diagram from your
  counts. There are three: **PRISMA 2020** (reviews), **CONSORT** (trials) and
  **STROBE** (observational). Each exports as an image. There is no builder for
  the STARD diagram of a diagnostic accuracy study; draw that one yourself.
- **Diagnostic 2×2**: enter index-test-vs-reference counts; Scholea computes
  sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, likelihood ratios, and the ROC/AUC.
- **Qualitative coding**: build codes from your data and cluster them into themes.
- **Reliability**: estimates **Cronbach's α** (internal consistency) for a scale.
- **Sample-size calculator**: built to the **WHO / Lemeshow** formulas, on the
  step where sample size matters (cross-sectional, cohort, case-control, RCT,
  quasi-experimental, survey, diagnostic, and animal studies). It defaults to the
  usual scenario for your design and offers all seven: estimate a proportion or a
  mean, compare two proportions or two means, case-control (odds ratio), cohort
  (relative risk), and diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity/specificity). Set the
  confidence level, power, allocation ratio and, optionally, a design effect,
  expected non-response, or finite population; the required n updates live and
  shows its formula and citation.
- **Feasibility check**: gauges how much has already been published on your topic,
  as a publications-per-year chart.
- **General statistics**: a full workspace of validated calculations: Welch and
  **paired t-tests**, **Mann-Whitney U**, one-way ANOVA, correlation, regression,
  **chi-square**, **Fisher's exact test**, **odds ratio / risk ratio / risk
  difference with CIs**, **descriptives**, and a **proportion CI**. Results
  **compute automatically as you type**; the right calculation for your study
  design is pre-selected (e.g. odds ratios for a case-control study), and you can
  switch to any other manually. Each result shows its method, citation, and
  assumption warnings.

Every statistical tool shows the method it used and its assumptions, and is tested
against reference values. Scholea only ships statistics it can stand behind.

## 7. AI-assisted writing

If you've added an AI key in Settings, Scholea can help you write **any text box**.

- **Click into a text box** (a short-text or long-text field, or a keyword tag box)
  and a small **✨ sparkle icon** appears in its corner.
- **Click the sparkle** and Scholea drafts the content for *that specific field*
  or, if you've already written something, **refines and tightens** it.
- The suggestion is **written in context**: it knows what the field is for, what the
  step is trying to achieve, your study type and reporting standard, and what you've
  already entered elsewhere in the step. So the draft for a "Primary outcome" box is
  short and specific, while an "Analysis plan" box gets a few well-structured
  sentences.
- The draft lands **straight in the box** for you to edit.
- The sparkle only appears when a text box is focused, and only when you have an AI
  key set. No key, no icon.

Some steps also offer a larger **"Refine my…"** button (e.g. *Refine my PICO*) that
suggests improvements to a whole step and shows them as a separate draft. Without
an AI key, that button opens Settings instead.

> Everything the AI produces is a **draft to verify**, never finished text.
> Scholea tells the AI not to invent data, results, or citations, and to say what
> is missing instead. An AI can still get things wrong, so check every statement.
> You remain the author and are responsible for the content.

## 8. Checklists, reviews & collaboration

From inside a project (header buttons and ⌘K):

- **Checklist** is a tickable version of your target reporting standard (PRISMA,
  STROBE, CONSORT, etc.). Tick items as you satisfy them; a live percentage shows
  how complete your reporting is.
- **Discussion & review** sits at the bottom of every step: comments, and a
  supervisor's sign-off.
- **Activity** is the contribution ledger kept on this device: who did what, and
  when. For a shared project, the whole team's activity is in **Share**.
- **Share** *(optional)* invites other people into a project. It needs an account,
  and the button is there only if accounts are available in your copy of Scholea.
  Solo projects never need it.

### Comments and sign-offs

At the bottom of every step you can leave comments, and a supervisor can sign the
step off with **Approve step** or **Request changes**, with an optional note. A
comment cannot be edited or deleted once it is posted.

**On a project kept on this device**, the first comment asks for your name and
role (Researcher or Supervisor). You can change both in Settings. Whoever chose
Supervisor on this device can sign steps off.

**On a project in your account**, the discussion belongs to the whole team:

- Your account name is used, not the name in Settings.
- Every member can comment.
- Only a project **Supervisor** or an **Owner** can sign a step off, whatever role
  is chosen in Settings. Viewers and Editors see a note that says so.
- A step has one sign-off at a time. A new one replaces the earlier one.
- What you write while offline is kept on this device, marked
  **Not uploaded yet**, and uploads when you are back online. Other people's
  comments arrive when Scholea syncs. Choose **Refresh** under the discussion to
  check now.
- If someone else signed the same step off while yours was waiting to upload,
  Scholea does not replace theirs. It tells you, and you can sign off again if it
  still applies.

When you add a project to your account, the comments and sign-offs already on it
are uploaded under your account name. A comment that was written under a different
name says so.

### Sharing a project

Sharing works by invitation, and the other person accepts inside Scholea.
**Scholea does not send an invitation email**, so tell them yourself.

1. Open the project and choose **Share**. If the project is only on this device,
   choose **Add to my account** first.
2. Under **Invite by email**, type the person's email address, pick a role, and
   choose **Invite**.
3. Tell the person that an invitation is waiting for them in Scholea.

The person sees the invitation on their Projects page, and under **Invitations**
in the account menu, once they are signed in with that address. It also works if
they create their account afterwards. They choose **Accept**, and the project is
added to their account and downloaded to their device, or they choose
**Decline**.

- Only an owner can invite people, change roles or remove someone.
- Invitations expire after 30 days. A project can have up to 20 waiting at a
  time, and you can send up to 30 a day.
- If someone declines, you are not told. The invitation keeps showing under
  **Pending invitations** until it expires. If someone cannot see their
  invitation, cancel it and invite them again.
- Only accept invitations from people you know.

Each person on a project has a role:

| Role | What the person can do |
|------|------------------------|
| **Viewer** | Read the project and comment. |
| **Editor** | Read, comment and edit the project. |
| **Supervisor** | Read, comment and sign steps off. Cannot edit the project. |
| **Owner** | Everything: edit, sign off, invite and remove people, change roles, and delete the project for everyone. |

You invite someone as a Viewer, Editor or Supervisor. An owner can make a member
an Owner afterwards. That cannot be taken back in Share, so Scholea asks first.

A Viewer or Supervisor can still type into the steps of a shared project, but
those changes are not saved to the shared project, and a notice at the top of the
project says so.

Scholea keeps the most recent edit of a project as a whole. If two people edit the
same project at the same moment, the later save replaces the earlier one, so agree
who is editing. Changes from other people arrive when Scholea syncs, not while you
watch.

**Leaving and removing.** Any member except the only owner can choose
**Leave project** at the bottom of Share. An owner can remove a member. Either
way, the copy on that person's device stays there and no longer syncs.

## 9. Compiling & exporting your paper

The final step of every study type is a **Report** step. Alongside it, **Compile &
export** (header / ⌘K) assembles everything you've entered into a manuscript draft
laid out to your reporting standard.

- **Live preview** of the assembled draft. It puts **every figure and table you
  generated** beside the section it belongs to: the study-flow diagram (PRISMA /
  CONSORT / STROBE), the forest and funnel plots, the per-study meta table, the
  risk-of-bias table, the ROC curve, and the publications-per-year chart. Any
  that has no section of its own goes under **Figures & tables**.
- **Figures travel with the export.** Word, Markdown and Print / PDF include the
  actual figures and tables. The LaTeX file marks where each one belongs, with
  its caption, and points to the Word or PDF export for the figure itself.
- **Citations**: format your reference list in **APA 7**, **Vancouver** or
  **Harvard** with the built-in formatter, or choose one of seven **CSL** styles
  (among them Chicago author-date, IEEE, Nature and MLA), which Scholea fetches
  from the internet when you choose it.
- **Export** as **Markdown**, **Word** (`.doc`), or **LaTeX**, or use **Print / PDF**.
- **AI-use disclosure**: a ready-to-adapt statement you can include, plus a reminder
  that the draft is *not* submission-ready.

> The flow diagram is generated from the numbers you entered along the way. If it
> doesn't add up, fix the source step. Don't edit the diagram by hand.

## 10. Choosing your study type

Pick the type that matches your **question and design**:

| Family | Types | Choose when… |
|--------|-------|--------------|
| **Evidence synthesis** | Systematic Review, Meta-analysis, Scoping Review, Narrative Review, Umbrella Review | You're synthesising *existing* studies rather than collecting new data. |
| **Observational** | Cross-sectional, Cohort, Case-control | You observe people/exposures without assigning an intervention. |
| **Experimental** | RCT, Quasi-experimental | You deliver an intervention and measure its effect. |
| **Diagnostic** | Diagnostic Accuracy | You're evaluating how well a test detects a condition. |
| **Qualitative** | Qualitative Study | You're exploring meaning, experience, or process. |
| **Survey** | Survey / Questionnaire | You're measuring constructs with a questionnaire. |
| **Mixed-methods** | Mixed-methods | You need both numbers *and* narrative, integrated. |
| **Reports & other** | Case Report/Series, Economic Evaluation, Animal/Preclinical | A single instructive case, a cost analysis, or preclinical work. |

Rule of thumb: *reviewing the literature* → an evidence-synthesis type; *collecting
your own data with an intervention* → experimental; *without an intervention* →
observational or survey; *understanding experiences* → qualitative. If the field is
young or messy, a **scoping review** maps it; if several good reviews already exist,
an **umbrella review** summarises them.

## 11. Study-type guides

Each guide gives the study's purpose, when to choose it, its reporting standard, and
for **every step**: what to do in the app **and how to actually carry it out**.
Fields marked *(advanced)* appear only at higher complexity levels. Remember you can
use the ✨ sparkle in any text box to draft or refine your wording.

---

### Systematic Review
**Reporting standard:** PRISMA 2020 · **Family:** Evidence synthesis · **12 steps**

A rigorous, protocol-driven synthesis that finds, appraises, and combines *all*
eligible studies to answer one focused question. Choose it when you need a
trustworthy, reproducible answer.

1. **Frame the question (PICO).** *In the app:* enter Population, Intervention,
   Comparator, Outcome, and a one-sentence question. *How:* write down your clinical
   curiosity, then split it into the four PICO parts, e.g. *"In university students
   (P), does an 8-week mindfulness course (I) versus no intervention (C) reduce exam
   anxiety (O)?"* If it's too broad ("does therapy help students?") narrow the
   population and outcome; too narrow and no studies will match.
2. **Feasibility & saturation check.** *How:* spend an hour doing a quick search of
   PubMed/Google Scholar and the **PROSPERO** registry. Count how many recent reviews
   already answer your question and roughly how many primary studies exist. If good
   reviews already exist, write down the specific gap yours fills (new trials, a
   different population); if almost nothing exists, the topic may be too new.
3. **Write & register the protocol.** *How:* before you look at any results, write
   down your exact eligibility rules, databases, and planned analysis, and register
   them on **PROSPERO** (free). This is your defence against unconsciously changing
   the goalposts later. Paste your final include/exclude criteria into the app.
4. **Build the search strategy.** *How:* list synonyms for each PICO concept, join
   synonyms with **OR** and concepts with **AND**, and run it in at least two
   databases. Use the search tool; save each search string with its date. Aim for a
   *sensitive* search (catch everything) and exclude later at screening.
5. **Import & deduplicate records.** *How:* import all results, then run
   deduplication so the same paper from two databases is screened once. Note the
   number removed: it's the first box of your PRISMA diagram.
6. **Title / abstract screening.** *How:* read each title/abstract and decide
   include/exclude against your criteria. Don't read full texts yet. Have a second
   person screen independently; the live **kappa** tells you if your criteria are
   clear. Discuss disagreements before moving on.
7. **Full-text eligibility.** *How:* get the full text of everything that passed.
   The app lists each of your screened-in studies with an Include/Exclude toggle.
   For every paper you exclude, record **its own reason** in the field that appears
   (wrong population, wrong comparator, no usable outcome…). These reasons populate
   the diagram, and the confirmed studies flow into appraisal and synthesis
   automatically.
8. **Data extraction.** *How:* design a form that captures study characteristics and
   the outcome numbers you'll need: means, SDs, and group sizes for continuous
   outcomes, or events/totals for yes/no outcomes. Pilot it on two papers, then
   extract (ideally two people independently) to catch typos.
9. **Risk-of-bias appraisal.** *How:* judge each study with the right tool (**RoB 2**
   for randomised trials), rating each domain low / some concerns / high in the
   appraisal matrix; the overall follows the worst domain. A body of high-risk
   studies can't support a confident conclusion.
10. **Synthesis / meta-analysis.** *How:* decide whether the studies are similar
    enough to pool. If yes, run the meta-analysis, read the **forest plot**, and check
    **heterogeneity (I²)**: above ~75% means a single number may mislead, so prefer a
    narrative synthesis. If pooling, random-effects is the safer default.
11. **Certainty of evidence (GRADE).** *How:* for your main outcome, start high for
    trial evidence and rate **down** for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness,
    imprecision, and publication bias. Record why you downgraded, and rate each
    outcome separately.
12. **PRISMA report & export.** *How:* write up using the PRISMA 2020 checklist; the
    flow diagram builds itself from your counts. Check it reconciles (identified −
    duplicates = screened, etc.), then export.

### Meta-analysis
**Reporting standard:** PRISMA 2020 · **Family:** Evidence synthesis · **9 steps**

Like a systematic review, but the **quantitative pooling** is the point. Choose it
when eligible studies measure a comparable outcome you can combine.

1. **Frame the question (PICO).** *How:* define PICO **and** the effect measure you'll
   pool: **standardised mean difference** or **mean difference** for continuous
   outcomes, **odds ratio** or **risk ratio** for binary ones. If studies measure the
   outcome very differently, a standardised measure (SMD) is safer.
2. **Protocol & analysis plan.** *How:* register the protocol and decide **now**
   (before seeing data) whether to use fixed- or random-effects, which subgroups and
   sensitivity analyses you'll run, and how you'll check publication bias.
3. **Build the search strategy.** *How:* as for a systematic review (sensitive and
   multi-database), and also hand-search reference lists and trial registries to catch
   unpublished results (a key defence against publication bias).
4. **Import & deduplicate.** *How:* remove duplicates so each study is screened once.
5. **Screen records.** *How:* dual independent screening; calibrate with the kappa.
6. **Extract effect data.** *How:* for each study pull exactly what your effect
   measure needs (means/SDs/group sizes, or events/totals) at the timepoint you care
   about. If a study reports medians/IQRs, plan a conversion. Extract in duplicate.
7. **Risk-of-bias appraisal.** *How:* appraise every included study for the outcome
   you're pooling; you'll use this in a sensitivity analysis (e.g. excluding high-risk
   studies).
8. **Meta-analysis.** *How:* pool the effects in the synthesis tool and read the
   **forest plot** (each study's effect + the pooled diamond). Check **I²** for
   heterogeneity and use the **funnel plot / Egger's test** for small-study bias.
   Optionally cross-check with `metafor`.
9. **PRISMA report & export.** *How:* report the flow, the pooled estimate with its
   confidence interval, heterogeneity, and your bias assessments.

### Scoping Review
**Reporting standard:** PRISMA-ScR · **Family:** Evidence synthesis · **7 steps**

Maps the **breadth** of evidence rather than pooling it. Choose it when the
literature is emergent or heterogeneous. Scoping reviews **do not** appraise risk of
bias or pool results.

1. **Frame the question (PCC).** *How:* use **Population, Concept, Context** instead
   of PICO, and write a mapping objective: *"This review will map what is known about
   … among … in …."* Scoping questions are deliberately broad.
2. **Protocol & eligibility (PCC).** *How:* register a protocol (e.g. OSF) and define
   what you'll include across PCC **and which source types**. Scoping reviews often
   include grey literature and non-research sources, so say so explicitly.
3. **Search (incl. grey literature).** *How:* cast a wide net across databases plus
   grey-literature sources (theses, reports, guidelines, key websites). Record what
   you searched.
4. **Import & deduplicate.** *How:* remove duplicates so each record is charted once.
5. **Screen for inclusion.** *How:* two reviewers apply the PCC criteria. Screening
   is usually more inclusive than in a systematic review.
6. **Chart the data.** *How:* instead of extracting outcomes, design a **charting**
   table that records source type, population, concept details, context, and key
   findings. Pilot it and expect the categories to evolve as you read.
7. **Collate, summarise & report.** *How:* summarise the map both numerically (counts
   by type, year, region) and by theme, relate it to your objective, and report
   against PRISMA-ScR. Describe what exists and where the gaps are. Don't draw
   effectiveness conclusions.

### Narrative / Literature Review
**Reporting standard:** SANRA · **Family:** Evidence synthesis · **5 steps**

A structured but **non-systematic** expert overview. Choose it for a broad
orientation, not when you need an unbiased answer to a focused question.

1. **Define the topic & importance** *(SANRA 1).* *How:* name the topic and, in a few
   sentences, justify why a review is needed now (new developments, confusion in the
   field, a teaching need).
2. **Aims & questions** *(SANRA 2).* *How:* state explicit aims so readers know the
   review's scope and boundaries.
3. **Describe your literature search** *(SANRA 3).* *How:* say where and how you
   looked (databases, key journals, snowballing from references). You needn't be
   exhaustive, but be transparent so readers can judge coverage.
4. **Structure & synthesise** *(SANRA 4–5).* *How:* organise the literature by theme
   (or chronologically), then **compare and contrast** findings rather than
   summarising paper by paper, and back **every claim with a citation**.
5. **Write & self-assess (SANRA).** *How:* write the review, then score yourself
   against the six SANRA items to catch weak spots. Distinguish well-supported
   statements from your own interpretation.

### Umbrella Review
**Reporting standard:** PRISMA 2020 · **Family:** Evidence synthesis · **8 steps**

A **review of reviews**: your unit of analysis is the systematic review, not the
primary study. Choose it when several reviews already exist.

1. **Frame the question.** *How:* state a broad question suited to summarising
   existing reviews across a topic.
2. **Protocol & eligibility.** *How:* register, define which review types qualify, and
   decide how you'll handle **overlap** when the same primary studies appear in
   several reviews (a citation matrix / corrected covered area).
3. **Search for reviews.** *How:* search specifically for systematic reviews and
   meta-analyses (filter your strategy to review designs).
4. **Import & deduplicate.** *How:* remove duplicate reviews.
5. **Screen reviews.** *How:* two reviewers select eligible reviews; record
   exclusions.
6. **Appraise reviews (AMSTAR-2).** *How:* rate each included review's overall
   confidence (high → critically low) using AMSTAR-2's domains (protocol registered?
   duplicate selection? risk of bias assessed?). Don't lean on critically-low reviews.
7. **Synthesise across reviews.** *How:* tabulate each review's population, effect
   estimates, and certainty; note where reviews agree and disagree. **Describe
   overlap** rather than re-pooling primary studies (which double-counts).
8. **Report & export.** *How:* report selection, AMSTAR-2 confidence, overlap, and
   your synthesis against PRISMA.

### Cross-sectional Study
**Reporting standard:** STROBE · **Family:** Observational · **6 steps**

Measures exposure and outcome at a **single point in time**: good for prevalence and
associations. It generally can't show that cause preceded effect.

1. **Research question & objectives.** *How:* decide whether your aim is
   **prevalence** ("how common is X?") or an **association** ("is X linked to Y?") and
   write the objective accordingly.
2. **Define variables** *(STROBE 7).* *How:* write a precise definition for your
   outcome, your key exposure(s), and likely confounders, including the exact
   instrument, units, and any cut-offs. Vague definitions wreck the analysis.
3. **Sampling & sample size.** *How:* choose how you'll recruit (random / consecutive
   / convenience) and justify your target N: a precision calculation for prevalence
   (expected prevalence + acceptable margin of error), a power calculation for
   associations. Record your assumptions.
4. **Data collection & ethics.** *How:* describe your instruments and procedures and
   how you'll limit selection and information bias. Obtain **ethics/IRB approval and
   informed consent before collecting any data**.
5. **Analysis plan.** *How:* plan descriptive statistics first, then your association
   analysis (prevalence ratios, or logistic/linear regression **adjusting for
   pre-specified confounders**). The step builds a STROBE participant-flow diagram.
6. **STROBE report & export.** *How:* write up against the STROBE checklist, including
   a participant-flow count and a table of participant characteristics.

### Cohort Study
**Reporting standard:** STROBE · **Family:** Observational · **5 steps**

Follows **exposed and unexposed** groups over time to see who develops the outcome.
It is the strongest observational design for showing time-order.

1. **Question & direction.** *How:* decide **prospective** (enrol now, follow forward)
   or **retrospective** (reconstruct a cohort from existing records), and state the
   objective.
2. **Define cohort & exposure** *(STROBE 6).* *How:* define who enters the cohort, the
   source population, and exactly how you measure exposure. Aim for exposed and
   unexposed groups that differ *only* in the exposure.
3. **Outcomes & follow-up.** *How:* define the outcome and how/when it's ascertained,
   your follow-up length, and confounders to adjust for. Plan how you'll measure and
   report **loss to follow-up**: differential drop-out is a major bias.
4. **Analysis plan.** *How:* plan incidence rates and **relative risks or hazard
   ratios**; where follow-up varies, use survival methods (Kaplan-Meier curves, Cox
   regression) and adjust for confounders in a pre-specified model.
5. **STROBE report & export.** *How:* report participant flow, how complete follow-up
   was, adjusted and unadjusted estimates, and a characteristics table.

### Case-control Study
**Reporting standard:** STROBE · **Family:** Observational · **5 steps**

Starts from the **outcome** (cases vs controls) and looks back at exposure.
Efficient for **rare outcomes**. Prone to selection and recall bias, so design
against both.

1. **Question & rationale.** *How:* state the objective and why case-control fits
   (usually a rare outcome that a cohort would need thousands of people to study).
2. **Define cases & controls** *(STROBE 6).* *How:* write an explicit case definition,
   then choose controls **from the same population that produced the cases**. Decide
   matching (none / individual / frequency) and the ratio. If you match individually,
   you must analyse it that way (conditional logistic regression).
3. **Exposure measurement.** *How:* define the exposure and measure it **identically**
   in cases and controls (ideally blinded to who is a case), using records or
   biomarkers rather than memory where possible, to limit recall bias.
4. **Analysis plan.** *How:* plan **odds ratios** with confidence intervals, adjusting
   for confounders with logistic regression. Don't interpret the OR as a risk ratio
   unless the outcome is rare.
5. **STROBE report & export.** *How:* report how cases and controls were selected, how
   exposure was measured, and your adjusted estimates.

### Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT)
**Reporting standard:** CONSORT 2010 · **Family:** Experimental · **7 steps**

Tests whether an intervention **causes** a change versus a comparator, using
randomisation to balance confounders.

1. **Question & hypothesis.** *How:* define Population, Intervention, Comparator, and a
   **single** pre-specified primary outcome with a directional hypothesis. One primary
   outcome avoids cherry-picking.
2. **Design & randomisation** *(CONSORT 8).* *How:* choose the design (parallel /
   crossover / cluster) and allocation ratio, then specify how the random sequence is
   **generated** (computer RNG, random blocks) and, crucially, how allocation is
   **concealed** so no one can foresee the next assignment.
3. **Blinding & outcomes** *(CONSORT 11–12).* *How:* state who is blinded
   (participants, carers, outcome assessors, analysts) and define how each outcome is
   measured. Blinding the **outcome assessor** matters most for subjective outcomes.
4. **Sample size** *(CONSORT 7).* *How:* run a power calculation for the primary
   outcome from the smallest effect worth detecting, α (usually 0.05) and power
   (usually 80–90%), and inflate for expected dropout so the analysed sample still
   hits target.
5. **CONSORT participant flow.** *How:* as the trial runs, record counts at each stage
   per arm: assessed → excluded → randomised → allocated → lost → analysed. These
   build the CONSORT diagram and must reconcile (allocated = analysed + lost +
   excluded).
6. **Analysis (intention-to-treat)** *(CONSORT 16).* *How:* analyse everyone in the
   group they were **randomised to**, regardless of adherence, with a pre-specified
   missing-data plan. Any per-protocol analysis is secondary. Switching to it because
   ITT "didn't work" biases the result.
7. **CONSORT report & export.** *How:* write up against CONSORT, including the flow
   diagram, per-arm results, and harms.

---

### Quasi-experimental Study
**Reporting standard:** TREND · **Family:** Experimental · **5 steps**

Evaluates an intervention **without randomisation**. Choose it when randomising is
impractical or unethical; controlling confounding is the central challenge.

1. **Question & rationale.** *How:* state the objective and pick a design: non-random
   parallel groups, single-group **pre/post**, or **interrupted time series** (many
   measurements before and after the intervention).
2. **Groups & assignment.** *How:* describe the intervention, the comparison
   condition, and exactly how units ended up in each. A concurrent comparison group is
   far stronger than pre/post alone for ruling out background trends.
3. **Confounding & bias control** *(ROBINS-I).* *How:* list the ways your groups might
   differ at baseline and your plan to handle it (baseline adjustment, matching,
   **difference-in-differences**, or propensity methods), plus how you'll account for
   co-interventions.
4. **Analysis plan.** *How:* plan an **adjusted** analysis (e.g. ANCOVA on change
   scores, difference-in-differences, or segmented regression for time series) and
   report effect sizes with confidence intervals. The general-statistics tool is here.
5. **TREND report & export.** *How:* report the design, how groups were assigned,
   baseline comparability, and adjusted estimates honestly.

### Diagnostic Accuracy Study
**Reporting standard:** STARD 2015 · **Family:** Diagnostic · **4 steps**

Evaluates how well an **index test** identifies a condition against a **reference
standard**.

1. **Question: index test vs reference.** *How:* name the test you're evaluating, the
   best-available reference standard you'll judge it against, and the target
   condition. Accuracy only means something relative to a sound reference standard.
2. **Design & participants** *(STARD).* *How:* recruit a **consecutive or random**
   series of patients in whom the diagnosis is genuinely uncertain, apply **both**
   tests to everyone, and have each test interpreted **blind** to the other. Describe
   this clearly (it maps to QUADAS-2 bias domains).
3. **Accuracy results.** *How:* cross-tabulate index vs reference into the **2×2**
   (true/false positives and negatives). Scholea computes sensitivity, specificity,
   predictive values, likelihood ratios, and the **ROC/AUC**. Report likelihood ratios
   too: unlike predictive values, they don't depend on prevalence.
4. **STARD report & export.** *How:* report the 2×2, accuracy estimates with
   confidence intervals, the ROC, and a STARD participant-flow diagram. Scholea has
   no builder for that diagram, so draw it yourself.

---

### Qualitative Study
**Reporting standard:** SRQR / COREQ · **Family:** Qualitative · **5 steps**

Explores **how and why**: experiences, meanings, processes. No inferential
statistics; the core is thematic coding.

1. **Question & approach.** *How:* frame an open "how/why" question and choose a
   methodology that fits: **thematic analysis** (flexible), **grounded theory** (build
   theory), **phenomenology** (lived experience), or **framework analysis** (applied).
   Note your stance on how knowledge is produced (reflexivity).
2. **Sampling & data collection.** *How:* use **purposive** sampling (choose people
   who can speak richly to your question), pick a method (interviews, focus groups,
   observation, documents), and judge sufficiency by **information power /
   saturation**. Get ethics approval and plan to anonymise transcripts.
3. **Coding & themes.** *How:* read and **code** the data line by line in the coding
   tool, then cluster codes into candidate themes; keep notes on your decisions (an
   audit trail). A second coder on a subset strengthens trustworthiness.
4. **Trustworthiness.** *How:* show rigour through **member checking** (participants
   confirm your reading), **triangulation**, a documented audit trail, reflexive
   memos, and **thick description** so readers can judge transferability.
5. **SRQR / COREQ report & export.** *How:* write findings grounded in **rich
   quotations**, state your reflexive position, and describe the analytic process (use
   COREQ for interviews/focus groups).

---

### Survey / Questionnaire
**Reporting standard:** CHERRIES · **Family:** Survey · **5 steps**

Measures **constructs** with a questionnaire, with a reliability check.

1. **Objective & constructs.** *How:* state what the survey measures and in whom, and
   name the construct behind each section. Prefer a **validated instrument** over
   writing items from scratch.
2. **Instrument design.** *How:* write clear, single-idea items (avoid double-barrelled
   and leading questions), match the response scale to the construct, and plan
   validity checks (expert review, cognitive interviews). **Pilot** it for
   comprehension before launch.
3. **Administration & sampling.** *How:* choose the mode (web / paper / phone), set a
   target number of responses, and plan how you'll compute the **response rate**. For
   web surveys, record whether it was open or closed and how you prevented duplicate
   submissions.
4. **Reliability (Cronbach's α).** *How:* after piloting, estimate **internal
   consistency** for each multi-item scale in the reliability tool. Aim for ~0.7–0.9;
   a very high α (> 0.95) can mean redundant items, not a "better" scale.
5. **Analysis & CHERRIES report.** *How:* plan descriptive and (if analytic)
   association analyses, and report response rates, the instrument, and reliability
   against CHERRIES.

### Mixed-methods Study
**Reporting standard:** GRAMMS · **Family:** Mixed-methods · **5 steps**

Combines a quantitative and a qualitative strand. The value is in their
**integration**.

1. **Rationale & design** *(GRAMMS 1).* *How:* explain why one method alone can't
   answer your question, then pick a design: **convergent** (collect both, compare),
   **explanatory sequential** (numbers then interviews to explain them), or
   **exploratory sequential** (interviews first, then a survey to generalise).
2. **Quantitative strand** *(GRAMMS 2).* *How:* outline the quantitative design,
   sampling, measures, and analysis. (You can run this strand as its own Scholea
   project and refer back to it.)
3. **Qualitative strand** *(GRAMMS 2).* *How:* outline the qualitative approach,
   sampling, data collection, and analysis.
4. **Integration** *(GRAMMS 3–4).* *How:* say exactly **where** the strands meet
   (design, analysis, or interpretation) and **how** you'll combine them. A **joint
   display** (a matrix putting quant and qual side by side per theme) is a powerful
   tool. State the meta-inferences that draw on both; two strands reported separately
   is *not* integration.
5. **GRAMMS report & export.** *How:* report both strands, the integration, and the
   insight gained from combining them.

### Case Report / Series
**Reporting standard:** CARE · **Family:** Reports & other · **5 steps**

A structured narrative of one (or a few) instructive cases. **Patient consent is
required** and identifying details must be removed.

1. **Why this case?** *How:* state clearly what makes it worth reporting (an
   unexpected presentation, a rare condition, an unanticipated effect, or a management
   lesson) and confirm you have **informed consent for publication**.
2. **Patient & presentation.** *How:* describe the **de-identified** patient, the
   presenting complaint with a timeline, relevant history, and examination/
   investigation findings.
3. **Diagnosis, intervention & timeline.** *How:* give the diagnosis and your
   reasoning, the intervention(s), and a **date → event timeline** (a CARE hallmark
   that greatly aids readability).
4. **Outcome & discussion.** *How:* report outcomes and follow-up, then discuss the
   case against the published literature and state the take-home lesson, framed as
   hypothesis-generating, not proof.
5. **CARE report & export.** *How:* compile against the CARE checklist, including the
   timeline and, where possible, a short patient-perspective statement.

---

### Economic Evaluation
**Reporting standard:** CHEERS 2022 · **Family:** Reports & other · **4 steps** ·
*advanced*

Compares the **costs and consequences** of alternatives.

1. **Decision problem & perspective.** *How:* define the options being compared, the
   population, and the **perspective** (health-payer vs societal; it decides which
   costs count), and the type: **cost-effectiveness** (cost per clinical unit),
   **cost-utility** (cost per QALY), or **cost-benefit** (both in money).
2. **Costs, outcomes & model.** *How:* list the costs and outcomes, set a **time
   horizon** long enough to capture the disease's consequences, choose a **discount
   rate**, and describe any model (decision tree, Markov) used to project beyond the
   data.
3. **Analysis & uncertainty.** *How:* compute the **incremental cost-effectiveness
   ratio (ICER)** and characterise uncertainty with one-way (deterministic) and
   probabilistic sensitivity analyses. A single ICER without uncertainty isn't
   decision-ready.
4. **CHEERS report & export.** *How:* report the perspective, horizon, discounting,
   ICERs, and uncertainty transparently.

---

### Animal / Preclinical Study
**Reporting standard:** ARRIVE 2.0 · **Family:** Reports & other · **4 steps** ·
*advanced*

Preclinical research with the **3Rs** (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) front of
mind. **Ethics/animal-welfare approval is mandatory before any work.**

1. **Objective & the 3Rs.** *How:* state the hypothesis and species and justify animal
   use under the 3Rs (why animals are necessary, why this number, how you minimise
   suffering). Confirm ethics approval.
2. **Design, animals & sample size** *(ARRIVE Essential 10).* *How:* describe the
   groups, **randomisation and blinding** of outcome assessment, and species / strain
   / sex / age, and justify the number of animals with a power calculation.
3. **Outcomes & analysis.** *How:* define the outcomes and statistical methods, and
   state how exclusions and the **experimental unit** are handled. If you treat a
   cage of animals together, the cage (not each animal) is your unit, which sets your
   real sample size.
4. **ARRIVE report & export.** *How:* report the design, sample size, randomisation,
   blinding, outcomes, and the 3Rs measures taken.

## 12. Data & privacy

- **Without an account, your work stays on this device.** Projects, notes and
  progress are saved in the app's storage on this device and nowhere else.
- **With an account, the projects you add to it are stored by the account
  service**, together with their comments and sign-offs and your name, institution
  and email address. That is how they reach your other devices and the people you
  share with. Projects labelled **This device only** are never uploaded.
- **The members of a shared project see each other's name and email address**,
  and a person you invite sees yours on the invitation.
- **Whoever runs the account service can read what is stored in it.** That is true
  of any hosted service. Do not sync information that identifies patients or
  participants.
- **Your keys stay on this device.** Search-source and AI keys are stored only on
  this device and are never synced. Scholea sends each key only to the service it
  belongs to. They are stored without encryption (see §5).
- **Signing out hides your account's projects on this device. It does not erase
  them from it.** On a computer that other people use, work in your own user
  account or browser profile.
- **Searches and AI help go where you point them.** Literature searches go only to
  the databases you ticked. AI help sends what it needs from your project to the
  AI service you configured, with your key.
- **A few features use a service whichever databases are ticked.** The
  feasibility check asks OpenAlex. Opening the abstract of a search result that
  came without one, and the AI appraisal, look the paper up by its DOI at Europe
  PMC, Crossref and OpenAlex. The optional cross-checks and the CSL citation
  styles are downloaded from public code libraries when you ask for them.

## 13. Troubleshooting

- **There is no Sign in button, and I am not signed in.** Your copy of Scholea was
  built without accounts. Everything except sync and sharing works the same.
- **The code did not arrive.** Check your spam folder, then choose
  **Send a new code** (it becomes available after a minute). If the address
  already has a Scholea account, no code is sent: sign in, or use
  **Forgot password?**, instead.
- **The code is refused or has expired.** A code works once and for a limited
  time, which the email states. Ask for a new one and use the newest: a new code
  replaces the earlier one.
- **A project says "Not uploaded yet".** Its content has not reached your account
  yet. It uploads the next time Scholea syncs: check your connection, then choose
  **Sync now** in the account menu. A project larger than 20 MB cannot be uploaded
  and stays on this device.
- **I was signed out and told my account is suspended.** An administrator
  suspended the account. Your projects are still on this device, but they do not
  sync and you cannot sign in until the account is reactivated. Contact the
  administrator of your Scholea service.
- **I was signed out without asking.** Your session ended. Sign in again: your
  projects are still on this device, and sync picks up where it stopped.
- **My projects disappeared after I signed out.** Projects from your account are
  hidden while you are signed out, not deleted. Sign in again and they are back.
- **I cannot see an invitation someone sent me.** Sign in with the exact address
  they invited. If the account menu shows **Confirm your email address**, do that
  first. Invitations expire after 30 days, so the owner may need to invite you
  again.
- **I cannot sign a step off on a shared project.** Only a Supervisor or an Owner
  of that project can. Ask an owner to change your role in **Share**.
- **A step won't let me continue.** Look for a red **error**: a required field is
  missing or invalid. Yellow **warnings** don't block you.
- **A later step is locked.** Steps unlock in order; finish the earlier ones first.
  Use **Continue** on the Projects screen to jump to the first unfinished step.
- **Literature search returns nothing / an error.** Check that the source is ticked
  in Settings. PubMed in the web version also needs its proxy address. Try another
  source: Europe PMC, Crossref and ClinicalTrials.gov need no key, and OpenAlex
  works without one, within lower limits.
- **The ✨ AI icon doesn't appear in text boxes.** Add your own AI key in Settings;
  the writing help is hidden until a key is set, and the icon shows only while a text
  box is focused.
- **My flow diagram doesn't add up.** It's generated from your entered counts. Go
  back to the step with the wrong number and fix it there.
- **Nothing seems to save.** It does: Scholea saves by itself each time you leave a
  field or move to another step. Leave and reopen the project to confirm your
  entries are there.
